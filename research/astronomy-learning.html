3D Astronomy Game-Based Learning Study

I studied where players got lost, spatially and conceptually, while learning astronomy through a 3D game.

  • STEM Education

  • Learning Sciences
Lab
Learning, Representations, & Technology Lab
Institution
University of Wisconsin–Madison
Role
Research Assistant
Year
2022

Research question

The lab studied game-based STEM learning and representational competencies using a 3D astronomy education game. The practical question behind my work was where the experience broke down for learners, and whether the breakdown was about the interface or about the concept.

Those two failures look similar from the outside. A learner who cannot find the next task and a learner who has misunderstood what a diagram represents can both stall in the same place, and they need opposite fixes.

What I was listening forNot whether participants liked the game. Whether they could navigate it, explain the idea back, and read the visual representations the way the design assumed they would.

Method and my role

I observed participants during study and playtest sessions and documented what they did and said while they worked through the game. I recorded behaviour rather than impressions, then combined it with the feedback participants gave in their own words.

Navigation
Where participants moved, where they backtracked, and where they stopped being able to find the next thing to do.
Verbal reasoning
How participants explained astronomy concepts out loud while playing, and where their explanation diverged from what the game was showing them.
Conceptual confusion
Moments where the difficulty was clearly about the idea rather than the controls, which is the distinction that decides whether the fix is instructional or interface work.
Visual representations
How participants used, misread, or ignored the astronomy-related visual representations the learning design depended on.

From observation notes to something the team could act on

Raw session notes are not useful to a design team on their own. I organised participant feedback and observation summaries into grouped usability and instructional-design issues, so that a recurring problem read as one item with evidence behind it rather than as six separate anecdotes.

The value of separating navigation problems from conceptual ones is that they route to different people. A navigation problem is an interface fix. A conceptual problem means the representation or the task sequence is doing something the learner cannot follow, which is a learning-design decision.

Output

Playtest summaries and synthesised observation notes for internal review and iteration by the research team.

Note

No quantitative results or study findings are published here. The data belongs to the lab and is not mine to release, so this page describes my method and contribution rather than reporting outcomes.
